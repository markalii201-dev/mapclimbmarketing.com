12 Things an Electrician Website Needs to Rank on Google in 2026

August 11, 202613 Min Read

A lot of electrician websites look fine and still never ring the phone. The difference is a dozen details that help Google rank the site and help a homeowner decide to call.

A homeowner flips a breaker and half the house stays dark. They search for an electrician, open two or three websites from the results and decide within moments which company feels safe to call.

Electrical work makes people nervous. They are letting a stranger into their home to work on something that can start a fire, so they look hard for signs that you are licensed, local and easy to reach.

Many electrician websites miss those signs. The phone number is hidden in a menu, every service is crammed onto one page, the photos are stock images and the site crawls on a phone. Google notices the same weak spots, and sites like that often struggle to reach the first page.

A good electrician website has two jobs. It has to give Google enough clear information to rank it for the services and cities you want, and it has to make calling you the obvious next step for the person who lands on it.

Below are 12 items that do both jobs, listed roughly in the order a visitor runs into them, with what to check on your own site for each one.

1. A Phone Number You Can Tap on Every Page

The phone is still how most people hire an electrician, especially for anything urgent. Your number should sit at the top of every page, in the header, where nobody has to look for it.

On a phone, it has to be a real link, so one tap starts the call. A number shown as plain text, or worse, inside an image, forces people to copy it by hand. Some will not bother.

Use the same number that is on your Google Business Profile. Matching contact details across your site and your listings is part of how Google confirms who you are, and it saves customers from calling an old line.

If you use call tracking to see which marketing brings in calls, set it up so your main number still appears in the code Google reads, or keep one tracking number consistent everywhere. Swapping numbers carelessly creates the kind of mismatch that hurts local rankings.

Put the number in the footer as well, and repeat a call button after key sections on long pages. Nobody should reach the end of a page and wonder how to reach you.

2. A Headline That Says What You Do and Where

The first line on your homepage should tell a visitor, in plain words, that you are an electrician and which area you serve. Something as simple as “Licensed Electricians Serving the Greater Springfield Area” does more than a slogan about quality or passion.

Under it, add a short line about the work you handle most, such as panel upgrades, rewiring, lighting and repairs, plus a call button. If you mostly work in homes rather than commercial buildings, say that too, so the wrong jobs filter themselves out.

That helps people decide in seconds whether they are in the right place, and it helps Google, because the main heading is one of the clearest signals about what a page covers.

Skip the rotating banner with three different messages. Visitors rarely wait for the second slide, and the moving parts slow the page down.

3. Designed for Phones First

Most people searching for an electrician do it on a phone, often while standing next to the problem, with a flashlight in the other hand. Google also looks at the mobile version of your site first when it decides how to rank it.

Designing for phones first means more than a site that technically shrinks to fit. Text should be readable without zooming, buttons should be big enough to tap with a thumb, and the most important information should come first instead of sitting under a giant photo.

Look at your own site on your phone the way a customer would. Can you see what you do, where you work and how to call without scrolling? Can you read the service list? Does anything spill off the side of the screen or overlap?

Menus deserve special attention. A long list of every service in a tiny dropdown is hard to use on a phone. Group services into a few clear sections, keep the call button visible outside the menu and test the menu on more than one device before you sign off on it.

4. Pages That Load Fast on a Cell Signal

A homeowner in a dark basement may be on a weak cell signal. If your page sits blank for several seconds, they hit the back button and call the next result instead.

Speed matters to Google as well. It measures real loading experience through what it calls Core Web Vitals, and pages that load quickly and stay steady while they load tend to do better than slow, jumpy ones. You can check your own pages for free in PageSpeed Insights. Look at the mobile results, not desktop.

The usual culprits on contractor sites are huge photos uploaded straight from a camera, background videos, sliders, piles of plugins and widgets that load before the page itself. Compress every image, size it for the space it fills, and cut anything that does not help someone call you.

Hosting matters too. Cheap shared hosting can make even a simple site feel slow. After any change, test the page again on your phone over cellular data, because that is how many of your customers will see it. A lean theme and a short list of plugins usually solve more speed problems than any setting.

5. A Separate Page for Every Service

One “Services” page with a bulleted list of everything you do is a common reason electrician websites do not rank. Google ranks pages, not companies, and a single page cannot be the best answer for panel upgrades, EV charger installs and lighting all at once.

Give each main service its own page: electrical panel upgrades, EV charger installation, whole home generators, rewiring, lighting installation, ceiling fans, outlet and switch repair, surge protection, troubleshooting and inspections. Only build pages for the work you want more of.

Each page should explain what the job involves, the signs that tell a homeowner they need it, whether a permit or inspection is usually part of it, what to expect on the day, and the areas you cover. Answer the questions people ask you on the phone, and close with a short list of common questions and a clear way to book.

Link each service page from your menu and homepage, and from related pages. A page about panel upgrades, for example, should link to EV chargers and generators, because those jobs often go together. That helps visitors find what they need and helps Google understand how your services connect.

6. A Page for Every City You Serve

Your Google Business Profile is tied to one location, but most electricians work across several towns. City pages are how you show Google, and the people searching, that you really work in those places.

A useful city page talks about that city. Mention the neighborhoods you work in, the age and type of homes common there and the electrical problems that come with them, like older panels in historic districts or new circuits in growing subdivisions. Add photos from jobs in that area and the services you do most there.

What does not work is one page copied ten times with only the town name changed. Google treats that as thin, repeated content, and it can drag down how Google sees the whole site. If you cannot say something real about a town, it may not deserve its own page yet.

Start with the towns that bring in your most profitable work and the ones closest to your shop, then build outward. Link each city page from a service area page and from your footer, and link from each city page back to the service pages that matter there.

7. License and Insurance Where People Can See Them

For an electrician, proof of licensing is not a nice extra. It is often the first thing a careful homeowner looks for, and some states require your license number on your advertising.

Show your license number in the header or near the top of the homepage, and again in the footer of every page. Say that you are insured, and mention any manufacturer certifications you actually hold, such as those for the generator or EV charger brands you install.

Keep it truthful and current. An expired number or a badge for a program you left is worse than nothing when a customer checks. If your license only covers certain kinds of work, be clear about what it covers.

These details help with Google too. When your website, your Business Profile and your listings all tell the same story about who you are, you add to the trust signals Google looks for in local results.

8. Real Photos of Your Crew and Your Work

Stock photos of a smiling model holding a wire stripper do not convince anyone. Homeowners want to see the people who will be in their house and the kind of work they do.

Use real photos: your team in uniform, your trucks, clean panel installs, finished lighting projects and before and after shots of messy wiring you replaced. They do not need to be professional. A recent phone photo in good light beats a polished stock image. Short captions that name the town and the job turn each photo into proof that you work in that area.

Get permission before you photograph inside a customer’s home, and leave out house numbers and anything personal.

Give each image a descriptive file name and alt text that says what is in the photo, such as a new panel installed in a garage. It helps people using screen readers, helps your images appear in image search and gives Google more context about the page. Compress photos before you upload them so they do not slow the page down.

9. Titles and Headings Written for Search

Every page has a title tag, the line that shows as the blue headline in Google’s results. On many electrician sites it says “Home” or just the company name, which wastes one of the clearest signals you can send.

Write each title around the service and the place the page is about, with your company name at the end. A panel upgrade page might use something like “Electrical Panel Upgrades in Springfield | Your Company Name”. Keep titles short enough to show in full on a phone.

Then give each page one main heading that says the same thing in plain words, and break the rest of the page up with subheadings that follow the questions people ask: what affects the price, how long the job takes and whether a permit is needed.

Write a meta description for each page as well. Google does not always use it, but when it does, a clear sentence about the service, the area and how to reach you can decide whether someone clicks your listing or the one below it.

10. Structured Data Google Can Read

Structured data is code that labels the key facts on your pages in a format search engines read directly. Visitors never see it, but it tells Google exactly what kind of business you are, where you work and what you offer.

Schema.org has a specific Electrician type, and your homepage should use it, with your business name, phone number, address or service area, hours, website and links to your main profiles. Add Service markup to each service page and BreadcrumbList markup so Google understands how the pages fit together.

If you have a section of questions and answers, mark that up as well. Keep everything in the markup consistent with what the page shows and with your Google Business Profile. Markup that claims things the page does not show can be ignored, or worse.

Many site builders and templates add generic markup or none at all. Ask whoever builds your site to set it up properly, then run each page through Google’s Rich Results Test and fix any errors it flags. Check again after big site updates, because theme and plugin changes can quietly break markup.

11. A Call Button That Follows the Visitor on Mobile

On a long service page, the header with your phone number scrolls out of view after the first screen. A sticky call bar fixes that by keeping a call button pinned to the bottom of the screen on phones as the visitor scrolls.

Keep it simple: one bar with a call button and, if you like, a second button for a quote request or a text. It should be easy to tap and should never cover the content people are trying to read.

Show it on phones only. On a desktop, the number in the header is enough, and a bar across the bottom just takes up space.

Make sure it does not clash with anything else floating near the bottom edge, like a chat bubble or a cookie notice. Two floating elements stacked on top of each other look broken and get ignored.

If you track calls, track taps on the sticky bar separately. It is an easy way to see how many calls start from your website and which pages produce them.

12. A Short Form for People Who Will Not Call

Plenty of homeowners would rather not pick up the phone. Some search during work hours, some want a quote for a project that is not urgent, and some simply prefer to type. Without a form, those people leave.

Keep the form short: name, phone number, address or town, and a box for what they need. Every extra field gives people a reason to stop. A dropdown for the type of service can help you route the request, but make it optional.

Put the form on your contact page and on every service page, close to the call button. Tell people what happens next, such as a reply the same business day, and then actually reply that fast. If you can, send an automatic text that confirms the request came through. A form that sits unanswered for two days is worse than no form.

Test it yourself every month. Submit a request, confirm the notification reaches the right inbox or phone, and make sure the thank you page loads. Forms break quietly after updates, and a broken form loses leads without anyone noticing.

How the 12 Work Together

None of these items is hard on its own. The difference comes from having all of them in place at once, on every page, and keeping them up as your business changes.

Some of them help you rank: service pages, city pages, clear titles and headings, structured data and fast pages give Google what it needs to show you for the right searches in the right towns. Others help you convert: a visible phone number, a clear headline, real photos, license details, a sticky call button and a short form turn visitors into calls.

You need both halves. A site that ranks but does not convert wastes the traffic it earns. A site that converts well but never ranks has nobody to convert.

Your website also works hand in hand with your Google Business Profile. The profile gets you into the map pack, and the website backs it up, giving Google more reasons to trust you and giving homeowners more reasons to call.

If you are not sure where your site stands, go through this list with your own site open on your phone. Note what is missing, fix the items that help people call you first, and then build out the pages that help you rank.
